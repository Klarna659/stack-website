Half-life explained in plain terms

A half-life is a simple idea with a few traps. Here is what it means, the arithmetic behind it, and why a single number cannot tell you when something is gone, using an imaginary substance.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-19

What half-life means

A half-life is the time it takes for an amount of something to fall to half. If you start with 100 units of an imaginary substance and its half-life is 6 hours, you have 50 units after 6 hours. After another 6 hours you have half of that, 25 units. The amount keeps halving at the same rate, whatever the starting amount.

The word comes from physics and chemistry, where it describes things that fade steadily, like a radioactive material or a drug being cleared by the body. In this guide we use one invented substance, called Substance X, so that nothing here can be read as being about a real product.

The arithmetic

The fraction left after a time t is 0.5 raised to the power of (t divided by the half-life). For Substance X, with a half-life of 6 hours:

0 hours0100%
6 hours150%
12 hours225%
18 hours312.5%
24 hours46.25%
30 hours53.125%
36 hours61.56%

Times that are not a whole number of half-lives work the same way. After 9 hours, that is 1.5 half-lives, so the amount left is 0.5 to the power of 1.5, which is about 0.354, or 35.4%. You can try your own numbers with the half-life visualiser, which draws the curve.

Why a number alone does not say when something has cleared

People often ask, "How long until it is gone?" A half-life cannot answer that, for several reasons.

  • Halving never reaches zero. Every half-life takes away half of what is left, so there is always a little remaining. "Gone" has to mean "below some cut-off", and the half-life does not say where the cut-off is.
  • The cut-off depends on what you are asking. The amount that matters for one purpose can be very different from the amount that matters for another. That is a question for a pharmacist or clinician, not something a single number settles.
  • A half-life is usually an average. Published figures describe a typical case. A real person may be faster or slower, and circumstances can change it.
  • It describes the amount, not the effect. How long something is noticeable, or how long it does anything, is a separate question from how long half of it takes to go.

The sensible reading of a half-life is as a rate: a way to compare how quickly two things fade, and to draw the shape of the fading.

What happens with repeated doses

Half-life arithmetic matters most when something is taken again before the last amount is gone. Say a dose of 100 units of Substance X is taken every 6 hours, which is exactly one half-life. Counting the amount just after each dose:

After doseLeft from earlier dosesTotal just after
10100
250150
375175
487.5187.5
593.75193.75

Each dose adds 100, and half of the total is gone by the time the next one arrives. The total creeps up and levels off near 200 units, which is twice a single dose. This is called accumulation, and the level it settles at is called steady state. After 4 doses the total is about 94% of the way there, and after 5 about 97%.

The same sum works for any gap. Call the fraction left after one gap r. The level just after a dose settles at the dose divided by (1 minus r). With a gap of one half-life, r is 0.5, so the level is 100 divided by 0.5, which is 200. With a gap of two half-lives, r is 0.25, so it settles at 100 divided by 0.75, about 133. A longer gap means less build-up, and a shorter gap means more.

Common mix-ups

  • Two half-lives is not "all gone". It is 25% left, not zero.
  • A longer half-life is a slower fade, not a bigger amount. Two substances can start at the same level and fade at very different speeds.
  • The half-life and the gap between doses are different numbers. The build-up depends on the ratio between them, as the sums above show.
  • Check the unit. A half-life can be written in minutes, hours or days. Convert before you compare two of them.

Using this sensibly

This guide is arithmetic about an imaginary substance. It is not a way to decide when to take, skip, delay or stop anything, and it says nothing about any real product. If you want to know how a half-life applies to something you take, ask your pharmacist or clinician, and write down what they say. Keep a record of what you actually took and when, because that, not a formula, is what they will ask for. A medication log is a good place to start, and the schedules explained guide shows how to write down a repeating gap clearly.
