How long will it last? Working out when a bottle, pen or vial runs out

Knowing the date something runs out is a few lines of arithmetic. Here is the method, with plain numbers, and what to write down so your next reorder never catches you out.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

The idea in one line

Days of supply is what you have, divided by what you use each day. Everything else in this guide is about getting those two numbers right and leaving some margin.

Step 1: count what you use per day

Work in one unit throughout, such as tablets, capsules, millilitres or doses. For a daily item, it is the number you take per day. For an item you take less often, divide by the days between doses. Three doses a week is three divided by seven, about 0.43 per day. A dose every other day is 0.5 per day.

If you are not sure how a phrase like "every other day" translates, our guide to schedules explained covers it.

Step 2: count what is in the container

Read the label for the quantity. Check the unit: the number of tablets in a bottle, the number of doses in a pen, or the volume in a vial. If the amount you take is measured in a different unit from the container, convert one into the other first. For a vial, the syringe units converter turns an amount in millilitres into units on a U-100 syringe.

Step 3: allow for losses

The number on the box is the most you could get. In practice some is lost: a tablet dropped on the floor, a few drops left in the bottle, liquid left in a needle or at the bottom of a vial, or a pen that needs a small amount used to prepare it. The leaflet says if a pen needs preparing, and your pharmacist can tell you what to expect. If you cannot find a figure, assume a small loss and round down. Counting fewer doses than you will probably get is safer than counting more.

Worked example: a bottle

A bottle holds 60 capsules and you take 2 a day. That is 60 divided by 2, which is 30 days. If you started the bottle on 1 October, the last capsule is on 30 October. You also dropped 2 capsules in the sink, so you have 58, or 29 days, and the last one is on 29 October.

Worked example: a weekly pen

A box holds 2 pens and each pen holds 4 weekly doses, so the box holds 8 doses. At one dose a week that is 8 weeks, or 56 days. If you take the first dose from the box on Sunday 4 October, the eighth is on Sunday 22 November, and the next dose due would be 29 November. That is the date you need a new box in your hands.

Worked example: a vial

A vial holds 10 mL. Each time you draw 0.25 mL, which is 25 units on a U-100 syringe. That is 10 divided by 0.25, so 40 draws in theory. If you take one a week, that is about 40 weeks. But a vial may carry a discard-after date once it has been opened, and the window is probably much shorter than 40 weeks. In that case the discard date ends the vial, not the arithmetic. Always use whichever comes first. See reading a vial or pen label.

Your own numbers will differ. The vial run-out calculator does the sum for the amounts you enter.

Step 4: add a reorder buffer

The run-out date is not the reorder date. Work backwards from it:

  1. Start from the run-out date.
  2. Subtract how long a refill takes: the pharmacy needs time to prepare it, delivery takes days, and the prescription may need renewing.
  3. Subtract a buffer for things going wrong, such as a back-ordered item, a holiday, a missed delivery or a delay in getting to the pharmacy. A week is a common choice, but you decide.

With a 30-day bottle that runs out on 29 October, a 3-day refill time and a 7-day buffer, you reorder on or before 19 October. Put the reorder date in your calendar or a reminder, not the run-out date.

If your item needs a prescription, remember that a prescription can run out of refills before the container does. Ask your pharmacist how many are left and when you need to see your prescriber.

What to log on a refill

  • The date you collected it.
  • The name and strength on the new label, in case it changed.
  • The quantity you received.
  • The lot number and expiry date.
  • Anything left from the old container, and whether you are using it first.

Compare the new label with your medication list. If the strength or the instructions have changed, update the list the same day and ask your pharmacist why.
