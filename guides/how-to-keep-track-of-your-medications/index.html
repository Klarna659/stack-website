How to keep track of your medications

Keeping track does not need to be complicated. You need one correct list, a way to see what you have taken and a date to reorder. Here is how to set that up with paper, a phone, a spreadsheet or an app.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-19

Why keep a record at all

Most people who take more than one thing rely on memory for the details: what it is called, how much, how often, when it runs out. That works until a busy week, a new prescription or an appointment where someone asks "what are you taking at the moment?" A record turns those questions into a quick look.

You do not need anything elaborate. You need one list that is correct, a way to see what you have taken, and a date to reorder. The rest of this guide is how to set that up.

What to write down

For each thing you take, whether it is a prescription, an over-the-counter medicine, a vitamin or a supplement, record:

  • The name, as printed on the label.
  • What it is for, in your own words.
  • The amount and how often, exactly as written on the label or by your prescriber.
  • When you started, and who prescribed it, if someone did.
  • How much is left, and the date you expect to need more.

Keep your allergies and an emergency contact on the same page. A printable medication list has a place for each of these.

Four ways to keep the record

Paper. A printed list on the fridge or in a drawer needs no battery and no sign-in, and anyone in the house can read it. The downside is that it goes stale. If you change something and forget to reprint, the paper is wrong. A printable medication log works well for a week at a time.

Phone notes. A note on your phone is always with you and easy to edit. It does not remind you of anything, and it is easy to end up with three versions in three places.

A spreadsheet. A spreadsheet is good for counting and dates: you can add a formula to work out when something runs out. It takes more setup, and it is awkward to use on a phone at the moment you take something.

An app. An app can remind you, log what you took and count what is left. The trade is that you hand your list to someone else's software, so it is worth knowing what to look for.

None of these is right for everyone. Many people use two: a printed list for appointments and something on the phone for daily use.

What to look for in an app

If you decide to use an app, these questions help you compare them:

  • Does it handle the schedules you actually have, such as every other day, once a week or every 8 hours, and not only "daily"?
  • Do reminders still arrive at the right time when you travel across time zones?
  • Does it show what is left and a date to reorder?
  • Can you print or export your list for an appointment?
  • Does it say plainly what it stores, where, and who can see it, and can you delete it?
  • Is the price clear before you enter anything, and does it work if you stop paying?
  • Can you correct a mistake in a log without starting over?

A five-minute weekly review

A record is only useful if it matches real life. Once a week, pick a regular moment and go through these steps:

  1. Compare your list with what is actually in the cupboard. Is anything missing, or anything extra?
  2. Look at the log for the week. Note anything you missed or took late.
  3. Count what is left of anything that is running low.
  4. Write down any question for your pharmacist or clinician.
  5. Update the list if anything has changed.

Five minutes is enough. The point is to catch a gap while it is small.

Refills: work out the date

The refill date is just arithmetic. Divide what you have by how much you use each day. Sixty tablets at two a day is 30 days. Then subtract the time it takes to get more: if a refill takes about a week to arrange, reorder when 7 days are left. The days of supply calculator does the sum and gives you a run-out date and a reorder date. For a schedule with a repeating gap, the schedule times calculator lists the dates. Before an appointment

Bring the current list, the containers or clear photos of the labels, and a short list of questions. Our appointment checklist has space for all three. The guide on keeping a medication list for your doctor goes into what to include.

Keeping it going

Tie the habit to something you already do, like brushing your teeth or making coffee. Keep the record in one place. Fix errors when you notice them, not later. If you stop for a few days, pick it up again instead of abandoning it. A record with gaps is still more useful than no record, and you can note the gaps honestly.

Not medical advice. This guide is about keeping records. It does not tell you what to take or how much. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about your treatment.
