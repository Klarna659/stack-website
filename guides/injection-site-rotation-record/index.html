How to keep an injection site rotation record

If you inject regularly, a short written record of where you injected makes it easy to see what you used last. Here is a simple way to keep one.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

Why people keep a site record

People who inject on a regular schedule often keep a note of where each injection went. The reasons are practical. A record shows at a glance which spot you used last time, so you are not relying on memory. It shows how much time has passed since you used a given spot. And if a spot becomes sore, red or irritated, you can look back and see when you used it.

This guide is only about the record. Which areas are suitable for a given medicine, and how often a spot can be reused, depend on the medicine and on you. Ask your pharmacist or clinician, and follow the leaflet that came with it.

Start with a clear naming scheme

A record is only useful if you can read it a month later. Before you log anything, decide how you will name a spot. Agree this with whoever teaches you to inject, so your names match the areas they have told you to use.

A simple approach is a short code for each spot. For example, you might use a letter for the side and a number for the position, so "L1", "L2", "R1" and "R2" are four spots. Write a one-line key on the first page of your record so the codes still make sense later. What counts as a spot is for your clinician to say. The record just follows their advice.

What to write down each time

Keep the entry short enough that you will do it every time. A good minimum is:

  • Date and time. Both, if you can.
  • The spot. Using your code.
  • What you injected. The name from the label, so the record still works if you take more than one thing.
  • Notes. Anything you noticed afterwards, such as redness, a bump, bruising, itching or soreness, even if it went away quickly.

If a mark does not fade, grows, or worries you, do not wait for your next appointment. Contact your pharmacist or clinician. A record helps you describe it, but it does not replace asking.

A simple grid

You can keep this on paper, in a notes app or in a spreadsheet. The layout does not matter. Four columns are enough:

WhenSpotWhatNotes
Mon 5 Oct, 9:00L1Weekly injectionNothing to note
Mon 12 Oct, 9:10R1Weekly injectionSmall red patch, gone by evening
Mon 19 Oct, 9:00L2Weekly injectionNothing to note

The spots in the example are made up to show the format. They are not a suggestion of where to inject.

Look back before you choose

Before each injection, glance at the last few rows. The questions to ask are simple. Which spot did I use last time? When did I last use the spot I am about to use? Has anything been sore there? If the answers raise a question, put it to your pharmacist or clinician at the next chance rather than guessing.

Reading back also helps you notice a habit. It is easy to drift towards the spot that is easiest to reach, and a record makes that visible without any effort.

Record the vial or pen too

The same note can carry a second detail: which pen, cartridge or vial the dose came from. If you ever need to report a problem, or if a spot reacts, knowing the lot number and the day you opened that container is useful. Our guide to reading a vial or pen label shows what to copy from the label and when.

If you use more than one medicine

Add the "What" column even if you inject only one thing today, because that can change. If you use more than one, check with your pharmacist or clinician whether they want you to keep a separate record for each, since instructions about spots and spacing can differ between medicines. A separate sheet per medicine is easy to read, and a single sheet with a "What" column is easy to carry. Pick whichever you will actually keep up.

Keeping it consistent

  • Log it straight after the injection, while you remember. A note written a week later is guesswork.
  • If you miss an entry, mark the row as "not recorded" instead of reconstructing it. An honest gap is better than a made-up row.
  • Bring the record to appointments. A clinician can read three months of rows in seconds.
  • If your instructions change, write the date and the new instruction at the top of the record and carry on from there.

Keep the record alongside your full list of what you take, so anyone reading one can find the other.
