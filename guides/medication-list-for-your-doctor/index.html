How to keep a medication list for your doctor

A current, written list of everything you take is one of the most useful things to bring to an appointment. Here is what goes on it and how to keep it from going out of date.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

Why a written list beats memory

Appointments are short, and the names of medicines are easy to forget when you are asked for them on the spot. Different people also see different parts of the picture: your family doctor, a specialist, a dentist, the pharmacist. A written list lets any of them see everything at once, and it means someone else can read it for you if you cannot speak for yourself.

A list is also a record of your own decisions. Looking back over it, you can see what you started, what you stopped and when.

What to write for each item

One line per item. For each one, record:

  • The name exactly as printed on the label. Copy it letter for letter, including the form (tablet, capsule, liquid, injection, cream, drops, patch).
  • The strength. The number and unit printed on the label, such as 1000 IU or 50 mg.
  • How often you take it. The wording from the label or from the person who prescribed it.
  • What it is for. A few of your own words is enough.
  • Who prescribed it. If you bought it yourself, write "bought myself".
  • When you started. A month and year is fine.

At the top of the page, add your allergies and reactions, and the name and phone number of someone to contact in an emergency. Put the date of the last update beside your name.

Put supplements and occasional items on it

Vitamins, herbal products, pain relief you buy without a prescription, creams, eye drops, inhalers and patches all belong on the list. So does anything you take only now and then, such as a sleep aid on a trip. They may feel too ordinary to mention, but ask your pharmacist or clinician which ones matter to them. They would rather see the item and decide it does not matter than never hear about it.

Write what you actually do

If you take something differently from the label, write down what really happens. If you skip it at weekends, or take half the usual number when you are busy, put that on the line. A list that describes your real routine is far more useful to a clinician than a tidy one that describes the plan. Nobody reading it is grading you.

Below the active items, keep a short "stopped" section: the item, the date you stopped and the reason in a few words. A stopped item is information too. A reaction you had last year is exactly what a new clinician needs to hear about.

Update it after every change

The list only helps if it is right. Make the update part of the change itself. When something is started, stopped, swapped or changed, edit the list the same day while you remember the details. Then change the "last updated" date so anyone reading it knows how fresh it is.

Every few months, hold the list next to your actual bottles, boxes and pens and check each line against the label. This is where most mistakes turn up: a strength that changed at a refill, or a bottle that is still in the cupboard long after you stopped it.

Take it with you

Keep a copy where you will not forget it. A printed page in your wallet or bag, a photo on your phone and a PDF you can send all work. Your pharmacist can usually print a list of what they have dispensed to you. Compare it with yours, because it will not include what you bought elsewhere.

Before each appointment

  1. Check the list against your bottles, boxes and pens.
  2. Add anything new, and move anything you stopped to the "stopped" section.
  3. Write down your questions at the top: side effects you noticed, things you are unsure about, doses you missed.
  4. Check the date at the top and update it.
  5. Print a copy or save it to your phone.
Not medical advice. This guide is about keeping records. It does not tell you what to take or how much. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about your treatment.
