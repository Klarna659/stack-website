Reading a vial or pen label: what to record

A label holds more than a name. Several of the details printed on a vial, pen or box are worth copying into your own records, and two dates on it mean different things.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

One rule above all

Follow the label and the leaflet that came with it, and ask your pharmacist if anything on them is unclear. This guide describes what labels usually contain so you can keep good records. It does not replace the instructions for your medicine, and labels differ between products and countries.

What is usually on a label

  • Name. Often a brand name and a generic name. Record the name as printed.
  • Strength. How much of the active ingredient there is, either per tablet, per millilitre or in the whole container. Check which one the label gives, because "100 per mL" and "100 in the vial" are very different things.
  • Quantity. The number of tablets, millilitres or doses in the container.
  • Lot or batch number. A code that identifies the batch it was made in. If a product is ever recalled, the lot number is how it is identified.
  • Expiry date. The date printed by the manufacturer for an unopened container that has been stored correctly.
  • Storage. The temperature range, such as refrigerated or room temperature, and sometimes "keep in the carton" or "protect from light".
  • Directions. How and how often to use it, as prescribed.

Two dates that mean different things

The expiry date and a "discard after" date are easy to mix up. They answer different questions.

  • The expiry date is about the container as it was sold: sealed, and stored as the label says.
  • A discard after or beyond-use date starts counting from an event, such as opening a vial, mixing a powder with liquid or starting a pen. After that event the label, leaflet or pharmacist gives a shorter window. It may be days or weeks.

So a vial can be well within its expiry date and still be past its discard-after date. The earlier of the two dates is the one that counts. If you are not sure which applies, ask your pharmacist before using it.

What to log when you open or mix one

The moment you open a new container is the most useful moment to write things down, because the discard-after clock may start then. Record:

  1. The name and strength, as printed.
  2. The lot number.
  3. The expiry date.
  4. The date you opened it, or mixed it, or started it.
  5. The discard-after date, worked out from the label or leaflet. If you are unsure of the length of the window, ask your pharmacist.
  6. Where it is stored, for example "kitchen fridge, door shelf".

A small sticker on the container with the opened date and the discard-after date saves you looking it up later. It is also worth taking a photo of the label on the day you open it, so the lot number and expiry date are on your phone if the box gets thrown away.

A worked example

Say a vial has an expiry date of 31 December 2026. You open it on 6 October 2026 and the leaflet gives a 28-day window after opening. The discard-after date is 3 November 2026, which is far earlier than the expiry date. A log line might read:

Name and strengthAs printed on the vial
LotThe code printed on the label
Expiry31 Dec 2026
Opened6 Oct 2026
Discard after3 Nov 2026 (28 days, per leaflet)
StoredFridge, in the carton

If your medicine has a different window, or if it is mixed from a powder, use the figures from your own label and leaflet instead.

Check the label at every refill

Labels change between refills. A different manufacturer may print a different name, a strength may change, or the directions may be updated. Each time you collect a refill, compare the new label with your last one and with your medication list. If anything differs, ask the pharmacist to explain before you use it, then update your records the same day.

Storage is part of the record

Storage temperature matters, so note where each container lives. If something was left out of the fridge, or sat in a hot car, do not guess. Write down what happened and for roughly how long, and ask your pharmacist whether it is still safe to use. Writing it down means you can give an exact answer when you call.

Tie the label to your supply

Once you know the strength, the quantity and the discard-after date, you can work out how long a container will last. A container can run out before its discard-after date, or it can reach that date with some left over. Both are worth knowing in advance. The vial run-out calculator shows the date a vial runs out for the amount you take, and the days of supply calculator does the same for tablets and capsules. Our guide to working out how long something will last walks through the arithmetic.

If you are turning a vial strength into a number on a syringe, that is a separate question for your pharmacist or clinician. Check your answer with them before relying on any calculator.
