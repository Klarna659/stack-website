Keeping a medication schedule when you travel across time zones

A schedule that is easy at home can get confusing when the clock changes. Here is what different kinds of schedules do when you cross time zones, and a simple way to plan ahead.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

Two ways to read a schedule

Every schedule is one of two kinds, and the difference matters when you travel.

  • A wall-clock schedule is tied to the time on the clock. "Once a day at 9:00" and "every Monday at 8 pm" are examples. The reminder fires whenever the local clock reads that time.
  • An interval schedule is tied to elapsed time since the last dose. "Every 8 hours" and "every 3 days" are examples. What matters is how much real time has passed, not what the clock says.

Within one time zone the two kinds look much the same. Across time zones, they come apart.

What happens when the clock changes

Take a daily 9:00 schedule and a trip five hours east. Your last dose was at 9:00 at home. At your destination, the next 9:00 is only 19 hours later, because the local clock jumped forward five hours. Flying five hours west instead stretches the gap to 29 hours. Nothing is wrong with the reminder; it did exactly what "at 9:00" means.

Now take "every 8 hours" with doses at 6:00, 14:00 and 22:00 at home. After a five-hour move east, the doses are still exactly eight hours apart in real time, but the clock times become 11:00, 19:00 and 3:00. The spacing stays the same and the clock times change.

Daylight saving works the same way on a smaller scale. On the night the clocks change, a "once a day at 9:00" gap is 23 or 25 hours.

Deciding what a reminder should follow

Ask one question about each item: does the exact spacing between doses matter, or is the routine what matters?

  • If it is a routine item, such as a morning vitamin, it usually makes sense to follow the clock where you are. Breakfast in a new city is still breakfast.
  • If the spacing matters, the reminder should follow the interval, and you may need to plan the changeover.

Which of these an item is, and how much the spacing can flex, is something your label, pharmacist or clinician can tell you. Do not guess, and do not shift doses on your own to fit a new time zone.

A simple plan for a long-haul trip

  1. Ask before you go. A week or two ahead, ask your pharmacist or clinician how they want you to handle the trip. Bring the schedule you actually follow, so the question is concrete.
  2. Write down your last dose. Note the date and time of the last dose before you leave, with the time zone, for example "Mon 9:00, New York".
  3. Write down the trip in local times. Note when you leave and when you arrive, each in the local time of that place.
  4. Write down the plan. For each dose during the trip, list the time in both your home zone and the new one. A short table on one page is enough.
  5. Keep it where you can reach it. Put medicines in your carry-on, with the plan and your medication list, and keep them in the original packaging.
  6. Log doses as you take them. The record of what you actually did is more useful than the plan if you need to ask for help later.

On the way home, repeat the same steps in the other direction.

A worked example

You take a once-a-week item on Monday at 9 pm, and your overnight flight from the US east coast to Western Europe leaves at 7 pm that Monday. The dose falls due while you are in the air. Your plan should say who you asked, what they told you and which local time you will take the dose, written down before you go. If you are on the plane and have no plan, contact your pharmacist or clinician when you land. Do not take an extra dose to catch up unless they tell you to.
