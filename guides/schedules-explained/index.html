Every other day, twice a week and weekly: schedules explained

Phrases like "every other day" and "twice a week" sound clear until you try to put them on a calendar. Here is what each one means, how to write it so nobody can misread it, and the common mix-ups.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-18

Two families of schedule

Almost every schedule belongs to one of two families, and mixing them up is the root of most confusion.

  • Fixed days of the week: "Mon and Thu", "every Sunday". The pattern repeats on the same weekdays, every week.
  • A repeating gap: "every other day", "every 3 days", "every 3.5 days". The pattern repeats after a fixed length of time, and it does not care what day of the week it is.

"Twice a week" and "weekly" are looser. They say how many, not which days. Pin them down when you write them.

Every other day

Every other day means one dose, then a day with no dose, then a dose, and so on. It is a repeating gap of 2 days. Over 28 days that is 14 doses.

The common mix-up is thinking it means "three times a week". It does not. Starting on a Monday, the doses fall on Monday, Wednesday, Friday, Sunday, Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday and so on. The weekdays rotate, so one week has four doses and the next has three. If you want the same days every week, you want a fixed-days schedule instead, such as "Mon, Wed, Fri".

Twice a week

Twice a week is two doses in each calendar week, but it does not say which days or how far apart. Monday and Tuesday are twice a week. So are Monday and Friday. If spacing matters, say it. "Mon and Thu" fixes the days and leaves gaps of 3 and 4 days. Over 28 days, twice a week is 8 doses.

Mon and Thu

This is a fixed-days schedule. The doses fall on every Monday and every Thursday, whatever the date. It is easy to put on a calendar and easy to say to someone else, which is why it is a good way to write "twice a week".

Every 3.5 days

A gap of 3.5 days is 84 hours. Two of those make exactly 7 days, so the schedule works out to twice a week, but the dose times alternate. A dose on Monday at 9:00 is followed by one on Thursday at 21:00, and then one on Monday at 9:00 again. Each dose is 84 hours from the last. Over 28 days that is 8 doses, the same as "Mon and Thu" but with an evening dose and a morning dose.

Weekly

Weekly means once a week, but there are two ways to read it:

  • On a day: every Sunday, whatever happened last week.
  • Every 7 days: seven days from the last dose, whenever that was.

The two agree until a dose is late. If your Sunday dose is taken on Monday, "every Sunday" puts the next one on Sunday, six days later. "Every 7 days" puts it on the next Monday. A weekly schedule is usually a day, not 7 days from whenever, but check your own instructions. Ask your pharmacist or clinician what to do when a dose is late, and write down the answer.

Other phrases worth pinning down

"Twice a month" is another trap. It means 24 doses in a year, on set dates. "Every two weeks" means 26. They sound alike and are not the same.

How to write a schedule so it cannot be misread

  1. Say which family. "On Mon and Thu" or "every 3.5 days".
  2. Add a time. "At 9:00" or "9:00 in the morning". A schedule without a time is only half a schedule.
  3. Add a start date. Gap schedules need a first dose to count from.
  4. Write what to do about late doses, using the answer from your pharmacist or clinician.

For example: "Vitamin D, every other day at 9:00, starting Monday 5 October" or "a weekly injection, every Sunday at 20:00".
