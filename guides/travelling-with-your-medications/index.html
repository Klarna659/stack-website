Travelling with your medications: lists, supply and times

A trip changes your routine, your clock and sometimes your supply. Here is how to plan the record side of it: a list, enough supply, a written time-zone plan and a log while you are away.

Written by the Stack team · Updated 2026-10-19

Start with a list

Before you pack, write down everything you take: prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and supplements. For each one, note the name as printed on the label, the amount, how often and what it is for. Print a copy and keep a second copy on your phone. If something is lost, delayed or needs explaining, the list is what a pharmacist or clinician will ask for first. The medication list template is a ready-made page.

Carry things the way they were given

Keep each item in its original container, with the label attached. A label shows your name, the name of the product and what it is. Photograph each label as well, so you have a backup if a container is lost.

Keep what you cannot be without in the bag that stays with you, not in luggage that could be delayed. Rules about what you may carry differ by airline and by country, and they change, so check the airline's and your destination's published rules before you go. This guide does not list any, because it cannot know which apply to you.

Work out a supply buffer

Take more than the exact number of days you will be away, in case a trip runs long. The arithmetic is simple:

(days away + buffer days) × amount used per day = what to take.

For a 10-day trip with a 3-day buffer, taking 2 a day: (10 + 3) × 2 = 26. If you take something every other day, count the doses instead: 13 days starting on day 1 is 7 doses, so taking 8 leaves one spare. The days of supply calculator will do the sum, and tell you when you would run out if the trip were extended. If you need more than your usual refill allows, ask your pharmacist well before you leave. Where you can, split the supply between two bags, so one lost bag does not leave you with nothing, and write down which bag holds what.

Write a time-zone plan

When the clock changes, a schedule written as clock times can stop matching your body or your day. Decide before you leave how you will handle it, and write it down. Whether to keep home time, switch to local time or shift in steps is a question for your pharmacist or clinician. Ask, then record the answer in your own words.

To turn home times into local times is just addition. Suppose you take something once a day at 8:00 am home time and you fly to a place 6 hours ahead. 8:00 am at home is 2:00 pm there. If you take something every 8 hours at 6:00 am, 2:00 pm and 10:00 pm at home, and you go somewhere 5 hours behind, those become 1:00 am, 9:00 am and 5:00 pm there. A written table of both columns, home time and local time, removes the guesswork when you are tired. The schedule times calculator lists the times for a gap, and schedules and time zones explains what intervals and daily reminders do when the clock changes.

Names and companions

The name on a label can differ from one country to another, so write down every name you know for each item, including the one on your own labels. If you are travelling with someone, tell them where the list is and what it says, so they can help if you cannot.

If your trip crosses the date line or you change zones more than once, keep the plan simple: one page with a row for each item, one column for home time and one for local time. Fill it in before you leave and keep it with the list.

Keep a record while you are away

Routines are easy to lose on the road. Keep a simple log: what you took, the time, and which clock you used, home or local. Note any dose that was late or missed. When you get back, a clear record makes it easy to talk it through with your pharmacist or clinician. A printable medication log fits in a bag, and a phone note does the same job.

Questions to ask before you go

Take these to your pharmacist or clinician, and write the answers on your list:

  • How should each item be carried and stored, and does anything need to be kept cool?
  • What should I do if something is lost, damaged or runs out while I am away?
  • How should I handle the time difference for each item?
  • Is a note from you useful to carry, and how do I get one?

When you are back

Count what is left, update your list, and note anything that changed on the trip. Compare the count with what the log says you used. If the numbers do not match, you have found something worth a comment next time you talk to your pharmacist or clinician.

Not medical advice. This guide is about keeping records. It does not tell you what to take or how much. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist about your treatment.
