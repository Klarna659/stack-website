Stack · mark, round 2

Five new directions, stress-tested the same way as the twenty before them.

The three-stacked-ellipses mark is rejected. This sheet does not re-litigate that round — it draws four new directions from a research pass plus one refinement of that round's own top pick, and runs every one through the same four gates that killed most of the last twenty.

One colourEvery mark is currentColor only — no gradient, no second value, no opacity trick standing in for one.
Legible at 16pxThe favicon size, and the size that kills clever marks. Every direction is rendered there, twice, on both grounds.
Names no substanceNo pill, no syringe, no vial — one of the five breaks this rule on purpose; see direction 04.
Promises no resultA dose tracker cannot imply "you will improve." All five pass this one.
What was rejected, verbatim: "doesn't stand out, doesn't look professional, screams vibe-code." The three horizontal bars before that were rejected as "lazy." Both notes point the same direction — generic geometry, no matter how clean, reads as generated. Every direction below tries to own one specific, describable idea instead.
01

Ascending Threshold

a dose crossing a rising line

What it means: one dot, one ascending line — a single dose shown as progress along a threshold being crossed. It reads the same for a vitamin streak, a titration schedule, or a lift log without naming any of them. Why it survives small: it's one thick mitred stroke and one oversized dot, nothing to blur — the dot is deliberately bigger than the stroke so the two shapes stay distinct as both shrink. Risk: at the smallest sizes the dot can still read as a flag pin stuck in a ramp rather than a marker riding a line — it is the most literal of the five and the least surprising.

02

Bracketed Dot

a dose held and watched

What it means: a crescent bracket cradling a dot — custody and attention in one glyph, which is the whole job of a dose tracker. Why it survives small: both shapes are solid fills with no internal detail to lose; it degrades gracefully all the way down to a fat comma and a dot. Risk: a crescent alone reads as a moon, or — worse — as the muted/low-battery glyphs it was explicitly disqualified for looking like in the prior round ("meniscus"); the dot in the pocket is the only thing that saves it, and it can never be recentred or dropped.

03

Fanned Dose Cards

doses laid out across time, not piled up

What it means: four equal cards fanned through an arc — a regimen spread out and legible, on purpose not a vertical stack of horizontal bars, which is Stack Overflow's silhouette, not Stack's. Why it survives small: the gap between cards is a knockout in the exact background colour rather than a second ink, so it still reads as one stepped silhouette rather than four thin outlines at 16px. Risk: it is the most detailed of the five and the only one load-bearing on that knockout trick — it only works on a flat, known ground, and breaks on a photo or a gradient.

04

Capsule S

high risk, high payoff — drawn on request

What it means: a capsule silhouette with an S-shaped seam cut through it instead of a straight one, so the brand's own initial is carved into the most literal shape in the category. Why it survives small: it is a single bold fill with one wide knockout band, so the S is still legible reduced, though the capsule's rounded ends soften first. Risk — and this is the one that matters: this direction breaks the repo's own mark rule, "must not name a substance — no pill," which the prior round used to disqualify a mark for the same reason ("column"). I drew it because the brief asked for it and it deserves to compete fairly, but my honest recommendation is to cut it on that rule alone, before it ever reaches a critic.

05

Mass

refined from the prior round's recommendation

What it means: the filled area under a decay curve — the app's own half-life measurement drawn as a shape instead of a chart. Carried over from the last round's recommendation and redrawn here with two optical corrections: a rounded peak instead of a hard right angle (so it stops reading as a y-axis), and a tail that goes tangent into the baseline instead of stopping at a corner. Why it survives small: it is the single boldest fill of the five — one curve, one solid mass, nothing thin to lose — so it is the last of the five to degrade as it shrinks. Risk: it is still one step from a generic area-chart icon and asks the viewer to already know what a decay curve looks like; its safety is also its ceiling.
